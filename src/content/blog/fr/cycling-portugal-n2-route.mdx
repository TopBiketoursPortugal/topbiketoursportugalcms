## Quick answer

The Estrada Nacional 2 runs 739 km from Chaves on the Spanish border to Faro on the Algarve coast, crossing the whole of Portugal from north to south on a single numbered road — which is why it has been nicknamed the country's Route 66. By bike it takes most riders eight to ten days at 75–95 km a day, with the hardest riding in the first third through Trás-os-Montes, the Douro and the hills of central Portugal, and the easiest through the Alentejo plains. Spring and autumn are the seasons to do it; summer in the interior is hot enough to be dangerous on the long exposed stages.

## What the N2 is

The N2 is the only Portuguese road that crosses the country end to end. It starts at a kilometre-zero marker in Chaves, in the far north-east, and finishes at the waterfront in Faro, passing through eleven of Portugal's eighteen districts on the way. For most of the twentieth century it was a main artery; since the motorways were built it has become a scenic alternative, and in recent years the municipalities along it have promoted it as a road trip, complete with a stamp "passport" that travellers collect at town halls and cafés along the way.

For cyclists, what matters is that it is an ordinary two-lane national road whose traffic has mostly moved elsewhere. It is not a cycle route and has no dedicated infrastructure. It is, however, a remarkably varied ride through the least-visited interior of the country, and it links several regions we run tours in. Our [cycling in Portugal guide](/guides/cycling-in-portugal/) covers those regions individually; this post is about the thread that ties them together.

## The route in sections

**Chaves to Vila Real.** The road climbs out of the Chaves valley and crosses the high plateau of Trás-os-Montes. Cool, open, sparsely populated, with long rolling climbs rather than steep ones. This is the remotest cycling country in Portugal, the same territory our [Trás-os-Montes and Douro International tour](/tours/bike-tours-portugal-douro-wine/) covers, and it sets the tone: you will be the only cyclist for hours.

**Vila Real to Régua and Lamego.** The N2 drops into the Douro Valley, crosses the river at Peso da Régua and climbs straight back out towards Lamego. The descent is the most spectacular kilometres on the whole road; the climb on the far side is the first proper test. If you only ride one section, ride this one — and consider the [Douro Valley bike tour](/tours/bike-tours-douro-valley/) if you would rather explore the valley than pass through it.

**Lamego to Viseu and Santa Comba Dão.** Granite country, pine and eucalyptus forest, a series of mid-length climbs and descents through Castro Daire to Viseu. Near Santa Comba Dão the N2 crosses the Ecopista do Dão rail trail, the subject of our post on [Portugal's rail-trail network](/blog/ecopista-do-dao-portugal-rail-trails/), and the same ground covered by the gentler [Coimbra to Aveiro rail-trail tour](/tours/electric-biking-coimbra-to-aveiro-loop/).

**Santa Comba Dão to Abrantes.** The forgotten middle of Portugal: Penacova, Góis, Pedrógão Grande, Sertã, Vila de Rei. Relentlessly hilly, with steep, short climbs through wooded ridges and river valleys, and the least infrastructure of the whole route. The roads are empty and the villages are small; plan where you will eat and sleep before you set off each morning.

**Abrantes to Montemor-o-Novo.** The road crosses the Tagus and the terrain relaxes into the northern Alentejo: cork oak, heat, long straight sections through Ponte de Sor and Mora. Big distances become easy; shade becomes precious.

**Montemor-o-Novo to Castro Verde.** Classic Alentejo. Wide horizons, wheat and olives, whitewashed towns spaced thirty or forty kilometres apart. Our [Alentejo wine country and castles tour](/tours/biketour-in-alentejo-wine-country-and-castles/) rides the eastern part of this region on quieter back roads; the N2 takes the direct line through the middle. Read the [Alentejo quiet-roads guide](/blog/cycling-alentejo-quiet-roads-guide/) before choosing between them.

**Castro Verde to Faro.** A final rise over the Serra do Caldeirão, the hills that separate the Alentejo from the Algarve, then a long descent through São Brás de Alportel to the coast and the end marker in Faro.

## How many days

Ten days at around 75 km is comfortable for a touring cyclist with luggage, allows a rest day, and keeps the hard central stages to manageable lengths. Eight days at 90–95 km is a solid effort for a fit rider on a light bike with bags moved ahead. Faster than that is a challenge event rather than a tour, and the central section will decide whether you make the schedule.

Whatever the plan, put the short days in the middle. The terrain between Lamego and Abrantes is where the elevation concentrates; the Alentejo lets you make up time later.

## Traffic and surfaces

The N2's traffic varies more than its scenery. In the far north and through the centre it is quiet, often very quiet; between Régua and Lamego, and on the approaches to Viseu, Abrantes and Faro, it carries local traffic and you will share it with lorries for short stretches. The surface is generally good — it is a maintained national road — but expect patched tarmac through the central hills and wide, fast, well-surfaced stretches in the Alentejo where speeds are higher. A rear light in daylight is sensible on the whole route; a mirror helps on the straight southern sections. Our seasonal [road surface and traffic notes](/blog/portugal-cycling-road-surface-traffic-report-autumn-2026/) give a more detailed regional picture.

## When to ride it

April, May, late September and October. The north can be cold and wet in early spring and again from November; the Alentejo and the central interior are dangerously hot between mid-June and early September, with afternoon temperatures that make the long exposed stages a risk rather than a challenge. Autumn has the added attraction of the Douro harvest around Régua and Lamego. The [month-by-month](/guides/cycling-in-portugal/#when-to-go-month-by-month) section of our guide explains the regional differences in more detail.

## Bike and kit

A road bike with 28 mm or wider tyres and low gearing is ideal; a gravel bike is equally good and more forgiving on the patched central sections. Carry two bottles and know where the next café is, especially south of Abrantes. Lights, a rear radar or mirror, sun protection, and — if you are self-supported — enough food for stretches where villages have no shop. In the interior, many restaurants close between lunch and dinner service, so plan the midday stop.

## Riding it supported

Riding the full N2 self-supported is a proper expedition, and many people want the experience without the logistics. There are two practical options. The first is to ride the best sections on a supported route: our Trás-os-Montes, Douro, Coimbra–Aveiro and Alentejo tours each overlap the N2 corridor for part of their length, with hotels booked, bags moved and bikes provided. The second is a bespoke itinerary: we design routes for small groups that follow the N2 where it is good and leave it where it is not, using our regular hotels and luggage transfer. If that interests you, the [self-guided bike tours guide](/guides/self-guided-bike-tours-portugal/) explains how the format works, and an enquiry with your dates and daily distance is the starting point.

## Is it worth it?

As a single ride, the N2 is less beautiful than the coast and less refined than the Douro; it is a road, with a road's compromises. What it offers instead is the interior of a country most visitors never see, a clear beginning and end, and the satisfaction of crossing Portugal under your own power. For riders who like a line on a map and a story at the end of it, there are few better weeks in Europe.
