## Quick answer

Portugal's ecopistas are greenways built on closed railway lines: flat, car-free, surfaced for bikes, and spread across the country from the Minho to the Alentejo. The Ecopista do Dão, running roughly 50 km from Santa Comba Dão to Viseu through forest and river valley in central Portugal, is the longest and the best known, and it forms the heart of our easiest multi-day tour. The network is the answer for anyone who wants to cycle in Portugal without sharing a road — families, nervous riders, e-bike tourers — and it links well with quiet country lanes for longer days.

## What an ecopista is

When Portugal's narrow-gauge branch lines closed, mostly in the 1980s and 1990s, the trackbeds were left in place. Over the past two decades a number of them have been converted into ecopistas — literally "eco-tracks" — with a tarmac or compacted surface, the old stations restored as cafés or rest areas, and the original tunnels and bridges kept. Because trains cannot climb steeply, the gradients are gentle by design, rarely more than a few per cent. Because the lines ran through valleys and between market towns, the scenery is often better than the road.

The result is a scattered network rather than a single system: each ecopista is managed by its municipalities, signage and surface vary, and they do not generally connect to each other. What they share is the thing that matters most to a visiting cyclist — no cars.

## The Ecopista do Dão

The flagship. The old Dão line ran from Santa Comba Dão, on the main Lisbon–Porto railway, up the Dão valley to Viseu. The ecopista follows it for roughly 50 km, climbing so gently towards Viseu that you barely notice the gain, through pine and eucalyptus forest, past granite villages and small vineyards of the Dão wine region, and along the river for long stretches. Restored stations mark the way; the larger ones have cafés and water.

The surface is tarmac for most of the length and is kept in good condition, with the seasonal caveat of leaf and pine-needle build-up in the shaded cuttings after autumn rain. There are tunnels, lit but cool, and a series of bridges over the river and its tributaries. It is rideable on any bike; a hybrid or gravel bike is most comfortable, and an e-bike makes the long gentle climb to Viseu disappear entirely.

We ride it on day three of the [Coimbra to Aveiro rail-trail tour](/tours/electric-biking-coimbra-to-aveiro-loop/), a seven-day, 230 km loop that is the gentlest route we offer — difficulty one on our five-point scale — and which uses the Dão ecopista as its centrepiece before heading over to São Pedro do Sul, Sever de Vouga and the canals of Aveiro. It is the tour we recommend first to families, to riders returning after a long gap, and to anyone for whom "cycling holiday" has always sounded like something for other people.

Riding the Ecopista do Dão independently is straightforward: Santa Comba Dão has a mainline station, Viseu has buses back, and the trail is well signed. Allow a relaxed day with lunch at one of the station cafés.

## The rest of the network

These are the ecopistas our routes use or that we regularly recommend. Lengths vary from a few kilometres to several tens; we have left precise distances to the municipal signage, which is the authoritative source and changes as sections are extended.

**Ecopista do Vouga.** In the Vouga valley around Sever de Vouga, on the old line that once connected Aveiro with Viseu. Green, steep-sided valley scenery, with the trail tracing the river. Part of the same Coimbra–Aveiro loop.

**Ecopista do Minho.** Along the Minho river between Valença and Monção in the far north, following the border with Spain. Flat, open, with views across the river to Galicia. It lies on the first day of the [North of Portugal and Minho tour](/tours/north-of-portugal-en/) between Melgaço and Valença and is one of the most pleasant stretches of riverside riding in the country.

**Ecopista do Tâmega.** From Amarante up the Tâmega valley towards Arco de Baúlhe, on the old Tâmega line. Wooded, gently climbing, with the river below; the Amarante end is a lovely half-day ride from a town that is worth a visit on its own.

**Ecopista do Corgo.** Out of Vila Real, on the line that once climbed from the Douro at Régua. Useful for linking Douro Valley riding with the Trás-os-Montes plateau without the main road.

**Ecopista do Sabor.** In the north-east, on the old Sabor line out of Torre de Moncorvo; wild, remote and quiet.

**Ecopista de Évora and the Ramal de Mora.** In the Alentejo, heading north from Évora through cork-oak country on the former Mora branch. Flat and exposed, excellent in spring and autumn, and a good car-free addition to an [Alentejo tour](/tours/biketour-in-alentejo-wine-country-and-castles/) for riders who want an easy day.

**Ecovia do Litoral.** Not a rail trail but worth mentioning alongside them: the Algarve's coastal cycle route, a mix of dedicated paths and quiet roads along the length of the south coast, which our [Algarve guide](/guides/cycling-the-algarve/) describes.

Other ecopistas exist and new sections open most years; the ones above are those we know well enough to recommend.

## Who the ecopistas are for

- **Families.** Our post on [cycling with kids in Portugal](/blog/cycling-with-kids-portugal/) makes the case: a flat, car-free trail with cafés every few kilometres is the safest introduction to touring a child can have.
- **Beginners and returners.** The [easy bike tours in Portugal](/blog/easy-bike-tours-in-portugal/) post lists the gentlest options; the rail-trail loop tops it.
- **E-bike riders.** The long steady gradients suit e-bikes perfectly, and the stations make charging stops easy to plan; see [e-bikes in Portugal](/blog/ebike-in-portugal/).
- **Mixed-ability groups.** A stronger rider can add an out-and-back on the road while the rest of the group stays on the trail, and everyone meets at the next station.
- **Anyone who hates traffic.** The obvious one.

## Linking trails into a tour

The limitation of the ecopistas is that they are islands. A tour built only on rail trails would be short and fragmented; a good route uses them as the car-free spine of a day and joins them with quiet rural roads. That is how the Coimbra–Aveiro loop is built, and how we use the Minho ecopista within the Minho tour. The [self-guided bike tours guide](/guides/self-guided-bike-tours-portugal/) explains how the GPS routes, luggage transfer and hotel bookings work if you would rather we did the joining-up.

## Practical notes

- **Surfaces** range from smooth tarmac (Dão, Minho) to compacted gravel on some of the newer or more rural sections; 32 mm tyres or wider cover everything.
- **Tunnels** on the Dão and Tâmega are lit but carry a front light anyway.
- **Water and food** are available at restored stations on the busier trails; on the remoter ones, carry your own.
- **Seasons:** the central and northern trails are shaded and pleasant even in summer; the Alentejo trails are best from October to May.
- **Access:** most trailheads are in towns with a railway or bus station, which makes one-way rides easy to arrange.

## The bigger picture

The ecopistas are one of the quiet reasons Portugal has become a serious cycling destination: they give beginners a way in, and they give touring cyclists a car-free alternative through exactly the inland regions where the roads are emptiest anyway. If you are choosing between regions and formats, the [cycling in Portugal guide](/guides/cycling-in-portugal/) sets the rail-trail loop against the coast, the Douro and the Camino so you can see where it fits.
