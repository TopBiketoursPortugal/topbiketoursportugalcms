The ride from Porto to Lisbon is the route we built the company around. It runs the length of Portugal's Atlantic coast, from the Douro to the Tagus, linking the Green Coast north of Aveiro with the Silver Coast that stretches down to Sintra. It is long enough to feel like a real journey, flat enough for a first multi-day tour, and varied enough that no two days look alike: river mouths and lagoons, dune boardwalks through pine forest, the giant waves of Nazaré, the walled town of Óbidos, the surf coast around Peniche and Ericeira, and finally the palaces of Sintra and the seafront at Cascais.

This guide is the complete plan. It covers the real stages of our [Porto to Lisbon bike tour](/tours/porto-to-lisbon-bike-tour/), with distances, terrain and the towns you sleep in; where to stay and what to eat; when to go; whether to ride guided or self-guided; how to reach Porto and get home from Lisbon; and what bike, kit and fitness you need. For an overview of every region you could ride in Portugal, start with our [cycling in Portugal guide](/guides/cycling-in-portugal/).

## The route at a glance

Between Porto and Lisbon there are two ways to go: down the coast or through the interior. The coast is the right answer for almost everyone. It follows the Portuguese section of [EuroVelo 1, the Atlantic Coast Route](https://euroveloportugal.com/en/route/eurovelo1), using a mix of dedicated cycle paths, wooden boardwalks over the dunes, quiet secondary roads and short stretches of compact dirt track beside the ocean. Inland routes show you vineyards and farming villages, but they bring more climbing, narrower roads with mixed traffic and hotter afternoons. The coast is flatter, cooler, safer and never more than a few kilometres from the next café.

Our full itinerary is **493 km over thirteen days, eleven of them on the bike**. Stages run between 27 and 68 km and average around 45 km, which at a relaxed touring pace means three to four hours in the saddle and the rest of the day for lunch by the sea, a beach stop or a walk around the town. The surface mix is roughly 60% cycle paths and cycling infrastructure, 30% low-traffic paved roads and 10% compact dirt close to the water.

The profile splits neatly in two. From Porto to Nazaré the route is essentially flat: the coast here is dune, lagoon and pine forest, and the only "climbs" are bridges and the short ramps up onto a boardwalk. From Nazaré south the coast firms up into cliffs, and the road rolls. The climbs are short but can be steep, with the longest pulls up into Óbidos, along the coast between Peniche and Ericeira, and from the ocean up to Sintra on the penultimate day. That is why we rate the tour **2 out of 5**: easy to moderate, ideal for recreational cyclists and beginners with a little preparation, and comfortable for anyone on an e-bike.

Two shorter versions use the same stages. The **Silver Coast Porto to Coimbra tour** (243 km, seven days) follows the first four riding days to Figueira da Foz and then turns inland to the university city of Coimbra, a good choice if you have a week and want a completely flat ride. The **[Portugal Atlantic Coast bike tour](/tours/atlantic-coast-to-lisbon-bike-tour/)** (394 km, eight days) picks up at São Pedro de Moel (or Figueira da Foz with the optional extension) and rides the more dramatic southern half to Cascais. There is also a 308 km, eight-day version of the main tour that stops in Óbidos and transfers to Lisbon or Porto from there.

One more thing in the route's favour: the wind. Portugal's coastal wind, the *nortada*, blows from the north and is strongest from late spring to the end of summer. Riding north to south puts it at your back for most of the way, turning the long exposed stretches along the dunes into rides where the kilometres pass almost unnoticed. Start in Porto, finish in Lisbon; do not do it the other way around.

## Stage by stage

These are the stages of the full tour as we run it. Day 1 and day 13 are arrival and departure days without riding. Distances are the ride distances of each stage; the hotels mentioned are the ones we use, with the Premium option in brackets where it differs.

### Day 1 – Arrival in Porto

You arrive in Porto, collect your bike, get it fitted and go through the route. Porto is worth a full extra day if you can spare it: the Ribeira waterfront, the port wine lodges across the river in Vila Nova de Gaia, the tiled São Bento station and, for a curiosity that cyclists seem to love, the cork museum at the WOW district. Night in a central 4-star hotel (Porto Trindade) or, on the Premium option, the 5-star Infante Sagres.

### Day 2 – Porto to Furadouro, 47 km

Leaving Porto you cross the Douro on the lower deck of the Dom Luís I bridge, roll through Gaia past the wine cellars and pick up the coastal path south. This is one of the best urban exits of any tour we run: from the Gaia seafront a near-continuous cycle path and boardwalk follows the beaches through Miramar, where the little chapel of Senhor da Pedra sits on a rock in the sand, to Espinho, a good lunch stop for grilled fish. The afternoon continues on flat paths and quiet roads to Ovar, known for its *pão de ló* sponge cake (two centuries of tradition) and fish and eel stews, and then to the beach village of Furadouro. Night at the Furadouro Hotel (Premium: Hotel Solverde Spa & Wellness Center in Espinho).

### Day 3 – Furadouro to Aveiro, 45 km

Today you ride the long sand spit between the ocean and the Ria de Aveiro lagoon on the N327, passing the fishing village of Torreira and the São Jacinto dune reserve, a good place to spot wading birds. The stage finishes with the ferry crossing from São Jacinto into Aveiro, the "Portuguese Venice", with its canals, colourful *moliceiro* boats and Art Nouveau façades. Visit the Jesus Monastery, look at the tiles at the railway station, take a boat tour of the canals and try the town's egg-yolk sweet, *ovos moles*. Night at the Hotel Moliceiro (Premium: MS Collection Aveiro – Palacete de Valdemouro).

### Day 4 – Aveiro to Praia de Mira, 40 km

A short, flat stage through Ílhavo, home of the Vista Alegre porcelain factory, to the candy-striped houses of Costa Nova on the waterfront, then south along the lagoon and through the dunes to Praia de Mira. Mira is a quiet beach resort with a lagoon behind the village; this is the evening to sit on the sand. Night at the Maçarico Beach Hotel (Premium: superior room).

### Day 5 – Praia de Mira to Figueira da Foz, 50 km

The Barrinha lagoon, fed by the Ria, starts the day, and then the route enters the great pine forest planted on the dunes, where the smell of resin mixes with the sea breeze. The forest paths are flat and almost car-free. You emerge at Figueira da Foz, the "Queen of the Silver Coast", where the Mondego river meets the ocean beside one of the widest beaches in Portugal. Night at the Sweet Atlantic Hotel (Premium: superior room). If you are doing the Porto to Coimbra version, tomorrow turns inland along the Mondego for 55 km to Coimbra instead.

### Day 6 – Figueira da Foz to São Pedro de Moel, 65 km

The longest stage of the tour, but still flat. The route runs south through Pedrógão and Praia da Vieira, fishing villages where the *arte xávega* – hauling nets ashore from the beach – is still practised, and then through more pine forest to São Pedro de Moel, a small resort of villas between the trees and the cliffs. The 7 km *Volta dos Sete* walking loop through woods, springs and beach is a pleasant way to stretch the legs; the local dishes are *sopa do vidreiro*, salt-cod soup and rabbit stew. Night at the Hotel Mar e Sol (Premium: superior room). This is also where the eight-day Atlantic Coast tour begins.

### Day 7 – São Pedro de Moel to Nazaré, 29 km

A deliberately short day so you have the afternoon in Nazaré. The approach along the cliff road is the first time the coast feels dramatic, and it builds to the lighthouse fort above Praia do Norte, where the underwater Nazaré Canyon – about 170 km long and up to 5,000 m deep, the largest submarine gorge in Europe – funnels Atlantic swell into the biggest waves ever surfed. Garrett McNamara's 2011 ride put the town on the world map; in winter you can watch the tow-in surfers from the fort. The old town below is still a fishing community, and the seafront restaurants display the day's catch at the door. Night at the Hotel Praia (Premium: superior room).

### Day 8 – Nazaré to Óbidos, 47 km

The route leaves the ocean for the first time. You pass the perfect shell-shaped bay of São Martinho do Porto, once an island before the Serra da Pescaria and Bouro hills were separated from the mainland, and then climb gently through farmland, vineyards and windmills to Óbidos, the best-preserved walled town in Portugal. Inside the walls, narrow cobbled lanes of whitewashed houses lead up to the castle; the town hosts a medieval market and a chocolate festival every year, and the thing to drink is a *ginjinha* cherry liqueur served in a chocolate cup. Staying overnight means you get the town to yourself after the day-trippers leave. Night at the Josefa d'Óbidos Hotel (Premium: Hotel Real de Óbidos).

### Day 9 – Óbidos to Peniche, 35 km

Back to the coast along the Óbidos lagoon, a shallow tidal lake separated from the ocean by dunes at Foz do Arelho and a natural stop for a coffee or a swim. Peniche sits on a rocky peninsula that was itself an island until the sixteenth century; it is one of Europe's great surf towns, with beaches facing every direction so that one of them always has waves, and it is the port for the Berlengas islands, a nature reserve 10 km offshore. The fort on the point and the fishing harbour are both worth the walk. Night at the Star Inn Peniche (Premium: MH Peniche).

### Day 10 – Peniche to Ericeira, 68 km

The longest and hardest stage, and for many riders the best. The coast road rolls continuously between clifftops and beaches: Baleal, Consolação, the surf beaches around Santa Cruz (home of the annual Ocean Spirit wave-sports festival) and on down to Ericeira. There is no single big climb but plenty of short ones, so pace yourself and use the beach cafés. Ericeira was the summer retreat of Lisbon families in the 1940s and 50s and is now a World Surfing Reserve, but the whitewashed old town above the harbour keeps its fishing-village character, and the seafood is excellent. Night at the Vila Galé Ericeira (Premium: superior room).

### Day 11 – Ericeira to Sintra, 27 km

Short in distance but with the biggest climb of the tour. The route leaves the ocean and heads up into the Serra de Sintra, where the air cools and the landscape turns to forest and granite. Sintra's hillsides of nineteenth-century Romantic palaces, gardens and estates are a UNESCO World Heritage Site: the Pena Palace, the Moorish Castle, the National Palace with its twin chimneys and the Quinta da Regaleira are all within reach of the town. Arriving by lunchtime gives you a full afternoon among them, and an evening after the coaches have left. Try a *travesseiro* almond pastry at the Piriquita bakery. Night at the Sintra Boutique Hotel (Premium: Tivoli Palácio de Seteais, a 5-star palace hotel).

### Day 12 – Sintra to Cascais, 40 km

The finale crosses the Sintra-Cascais Natural Park. From Sintra the road climbs through the forest and then drops to the wild west coast: Praia da Adraga, Praia Grande, Cabo da Roca – the westernmost point of mainland Europe – and the dune-backed sweep of Guincho beach, before the coast road curves into Cascais. Once a fishing village and then the summer residence of the Portuguese royal family, Cascais is an elegant resort with a marina, a citadel and a promenade that runs all the way to Estoril. Night at the Pestana Cascais (Premium: Hotel Cidadela Cascais, inside the old fortress).

### Day 13 – Transfer to Lisbon

A transfer takes you the 30 km into Lisbon. If you would rather ride it, the Marginal cycle path runs along the Tagus from Cascais through Estoril and Oeiras to Belém and the city centre; see our post on [cycling in Lisbon](/blog/cycling-in-lisbon-an-adventure-not-to-be-missed/) for what to do once you arrive.

## Where to stay

Accommodation is half of a cycling holiday. After a day on the bike you want a hotel that is on the route, has somewhere safe to put the bike, is a short walk from dinner and, ideally, looks at the sea. That is the list we select against: comfort and cleanliness after a day of cycling, a location near the route or the town centre, proximity to the coast or the scenery, and local character rather than a chain. Along the Porto to Lisbon route that means a curated mix of 3- and 4-star hotels, with a **Premium option** that upgrades to 5-star properties in the cities and superior rooms everywhere else.

The hotels we currently use, north to south:

- **Porto** – Porto Trindade Hotel, central and a short walk from the Ribeira; Premium: Infante Sagres, the city's classic 5-star.
- **Furadouro** – Furadouro Hotel, by the beach and promenade; Premium: Hotel Solverde Spa & Wellness Center in Espinho.
- **Aveiro** – Hotel Moliceiro, on the central canal; Premium: MS Collection Aveiro – Palacete de Valdemouro, a restored town palace.
- **Praia de Mira** – Maçarico Beach Hotel, on the beachfront.
- **Figueira da Foz** – Sweet Atlantic Hotel, on the seafront avenue.
- **São Pedro de Moel** – Hotel Mar e Sol, a traditional seaside hotel between the pines and the cliffs.
- **Nazaré** – Hotel Praia, on the beach in the lower town.
- **Óbidos** – Josefa d'Óbidos Hotel, just below the walls; Premium: Hotel Real de Óbidos, a manor house beside the castle.
- **Peniche** – Star Inn Peniche (3-star); Premium: MH Peniche, a 4-star by the harbour.
- **Ericeira** – Vila Galé Ericeira, on the clifftop above the beach.
- **Sintra** – Sintra Boutique Hotel in the old town; Premium: Tivoli Palácio de Seteais, an eighteenth-century palace and one of the Leading Hotels of the World.
- **Cascais** – Pestana Cascais on the waterfront; Premium: Hotel Cidadela Cascais, inside the seventeenth-century fortress.

All rooms are private doubles or twins with private bathroom and breakfast; single rooms are available on request with a supplement. Luggage goes ahead of you by van each morning and is waiting in your room when you arrive, so you ride with nothing more than a day bag.

If you are organising the trip yourself, the same principles apply. Book the small towns early in spring and autumn – Nazaré, Óbidos and Ericeira have limited beds and fill with weekend visitors from Lisbon – and ask every hotel where the bike sleeps before you confirm. In Óbidos and Sintra, stay inside or immediately beside the historic centre so that you see them in the evening, which is when they are at their best.

## What to eat

The Porto to Lisbon route is one of the rare cycling routes where nutrition feels indulgent rather than functional. You ride through fishing villages where the restaurant serves what came off the boat that morning, and the day's rhythm – coffee and a pastry mid-morning, a long lunch by the sea, dinner with local wine – is as much a part of the holiday as the riding.

A typical day looks like this. **Breakfast** at the hotel is bread rolls, cheese, ham, yoghurt, eggs, fruit, coffee and juice. A **mid-ride stop** at a café for an espresso and a pastry – the *pastel de nata* is everywhere, and every town has its own speciality. **Lunch** is light: soup, a salad, or a piece of grilled fish. An **afternoon break** for coffee and something sweet, and then a proper **dinner** with wine.

The specialities change as you ride south:

- **Porto** – the *francesinha*, a meat-and-cheese sandwich drowned in beer-and-tomato sauce, is the city's heavyweight; save it for the night before you ride, or the night after.
- **Espinho and Ovar** – grilled fish on the seafront at Espinho; in Ovar, the famous *pão de ló* sponge cake, convent sweets, and fish or eel stews.
- **Aveiro** – *ovos moles*, egg-yolk and sugar sweets in thin wafer shells shaped like fish and shells, and the sparkling and red wines of nearby Bairrada.
- **Figueira da Foz and the pine coast** – seafood rice (*arroz de marisco*), *caldeirada* fish stew, and at São Pedro de Moel the *sopa do vidreiro*, cod soup and rabbit stew.
- **Nazaré** – the seafood capital of the route: grilled sea bass (*robalo*) and bream (*dourada*), octopus rice, *caldeirada*, seafood platters and, if you are brave, *percebes*, the gooseneck barnacles prised off the rocks by hand.
- **Óbidos** – *ginjinha* cherry liqueur in a chocolate cup.
- **Peniche and Ericeira** – octopus *à lagareiro* (roasted with garlic, olive oil and potatoes), clams *à Bulhão Pato* (garlic, white wine, coriander), grilled sardines in summer, and the lobster and crab that Ericeira is known for.
- **Sintra** – *travesseiros* (puff-pastry pillows filled with almond cream) and *queijadas* (small cheese tarts).
- **Cascais and Lisbon** – *bacalhau à Brás* (salt cod with egg and shoestring potatoes), and the original *pastéis de Belém* on the way into the city.

Soup deserves a special mention. Portuguese meals almost always start with one – *caldo verde* with kale, potato and *chouriço* is the classic – and for a cyclist it is the easiest, healthiest lunch on the route. Fresh fruit (oranges, figs, peaches in summer), cheese sandwiches and nuts from any grocery make good jersey-pocket food for the longer stages.

Wine follows the route too: light, slightly fizzy *Vinho Verde* near Porto, Bairrada around Aveiro, then the whites and reds of the Lisbon region as you approach the capital. Most of our hotels keep a good local list.

Vegetarians and vegans will find it easier than the cuisine's reputation suggests. Vegetable soup is on every menu, as are salads, omelettes, cheese and grilled vegetables, and the bigger towns – Aveiro, Figueira, Peniche, Ericeira, Sintra, Cascais – all have dedicated vegetarian restaurants now. Tell us when you book and we will flag it to the hotels.

## When to ride it

The Atlantic moderates this coast all year. Summers are cooler than inland Portugal, winters are mild, and the route can technically be ridden in any month. But some months are clearly better than others.

**April to June** is our favourite window. Days are long and bright, temperatures sit in the high teens to mid-twenties, the dunes and the Sintra hills are green and in flower, and the towns are busy without being crowded. This is when Sérgio re-rides the route each year.

**September and October** are the other sweet spot: the sea is at its warmest, the summer crowds have gone home, the light is golden and the wind has eased. Early October is as good as it gets on the Silver Coast.

**July and August** work, with two caveats. The *nortada* is at its strongest – a help if you are riding south, but it makes the beaches chilly in the afternoon – and Nazaré, Peniche, Ericeira and Cascais fill with Portuguese and Spanish holidaymakers, so hotels must be booked well ahead and cost more (our high-season supplement applies). Start early, ride in the morning and spend the afternoons in the water.

**November to March** is quieter and cheaper, the winter swells at Nazaré are spectacular, and there are plenty of mild, sunny days. But rain comes in multi-day Atlantic fronts, daylight is short, and some beach hotels and restaurants close. If you ride in winter, build in a spare day and pack proper rain kit. For the full seasonal picture, see our post on the [best time to cycle in Portugal](/blog/best-time-to-cycle-in-portugal/).

## Guided or self-guided

The Porto to Lisbon tour runs in both formats, on the same route with the same hotels, and the choice is mostly about how you like to travel. Our post on [guided vs self-guided bike tours in Portugal](/blog/guided-vs-self-guided-bike-tours-portugal/) goes into the general trade-offs; here is how they play out on this specific route.

**Self-guided** suits this route particularly well, which is why most of our guests choose it. Navigation is straightforward – for long stretches you simply keep the ocean on your right – and you get a GPS unit loaded with each day's track, a road book, luggage transfers, all hotels booked, and a phone number that is answered. You choose your start date, ride at your own pace, stop where you like and linger where you like. From one person. Mechanical assistance and personal accident and liability insurance are included, and with eight or more riders a support van follows with water and snacks. Groups run on set dates with a minimum of four participants. It is the better choice if you are nervous about navigating, are travelling alone and want company, or simply want a fully looked-after holiday.

**Premium** is an add-on to either format rather than a third option: the same stages, but with the 5-star hotels in Porto, Espinho, Aveiro, Óbidos, Peniche, Sintra and Cascais and superior rooms elsewhere. For couples marking an occasion, or anyone who values a palace in Sintra over a saving, it turns a good tour into a memorable one.

## Getting to Porto and back from Lisbon

The route's great logistical advantage is that it starts and ends in Portugal's two main airports, with a fast train between them. You fly into one, fly out of the other, and never have to double back.

**Getting to Porto.** Porto airport (OPO) has direct flights from most of Europe and seasonal links from North America. The metro runs from the terminal into the city in about half an hour, or a taxi or Uber takes twenty minutes; our tour includes airport transfers. If you arrive in Lisbon instead, the Alfa Pendular train takes under three hours to Porto Campanhã and is the easiest way north. We recommend arriving a day early: Porto is one of Europe's great small cities and it would be a shame to see only the hotel.

**Getting back from Lisbon.** After the final night in Cascais, a transfer to Lisbon is included. Lisbon airport (LIS) is the country's largest hub, with direct flights to the UK, Europe and the US east coast. If you need to return to Porto – for a car, or a flight home – the train from Lisbon Santa Apolónia or Oriente takes under three hours, and we can arrange tickets or a private transfer; ask when you book.

**If things go wrong mid-route.** The Porto–Lisbon railway line runs parallel to the coast for the whole route, rarely more than 20 km inland, with stations at Espinho, Ovar, Aveiro, Figueira da Foz (via the Mondego branch), Caldas da Rainha near Óbidos, Torres Vedras, Sintra and Cascais. Regional trains carry bikes free; the Alfa Pendular and Intercidades need an advance bike reservation. Weather, a mechanical or tired legs can all be solved with a train and a phone call.

**Bikes.** Most guests rent from us – touring bikes or e-bikes, fitted in Porto and collected in Cascais, so there is no need to get a bike back north. If you bring your own, both airports handle bike boxes routinely and we can store the box in Porto and forward it to Lisbon.

## Bike, gear and training

**The bike.** A hybrid or touring bike with flat bars, 35–40 mm tyres and a comfortable saddle is ideal: the surface is mostly tarmac and smooth boardwalk, but there are cobbles in the old towns, the occasional stretch of compact dirt near the dunes and boardwalk joints that reward a bit of tyre volume. A road bike with 28 mm tyres will cope; a mountain bike is more than you need. Our rental fleet is exactly this kind of hybrid, with a rack and a bar bag.

**E-bikes.** Pedal-assist has changed who can do this ride. It softens the rolling coast south of Nazaré, makes the cobbled climbs in Óbidos and Sintra trivial, and lets partners or friends of different fitness ride together at the same pace. Every stage is well within a single charge, and every hotel has a socket. If you have not ridden 40 km in a day before, or you are unsure about the climbs, take the e-bike.

**Clothing and kit.** With luggage going ahead by van, what you carry each day is small: padded shorts, a jersey or wicking top, a light wind or rain layer (the coast can turn in an hour), sunscreen, sunglasses, a refillable bottle or two, a phone, and your GPS. In the bag that travels: comfortable shoes and clothes for evenings, swimwear, a warm layer for Sintra and for evenings by the ocean, and a second pair of cycling shorts. Mudguards are worth having in spring and autumn. Helmets are strongly recommended and are supplied with rental bikes.

**Navigation.** Self-guided riders get a GPS unit with the daily tracks loaded. Load the GPX files onto your phone as well (Komoot, Ride with GPS or Garmin Connect all work) as a backup, and download offline maps for the Silver Coast – signal is fine in towns but patchy in the pine forest.

**Training.** You do not need to be an athlete. If you can ride 30–40 km comfortably at home, you can ride this tour. The best preparation is simply regular riding in the two months before: two or three rides a week, building one of them up to 50–60 km, ideally with a couple of back-to-back days so your body is used to riding again the morning after. Include a few hills if you are riding without assistance – short, steep ones, which is what the southern half throws at you. Test your saddle and shorts on those long rides; saddle comfort, not fitness, is what makes or breaks a multi-day tour.

**Safety on the road.** Portuguese drivers are generally courteous to cyclists, and the route is designed to keep you off busy roads. Ride predictably, signal clearly, use lights if you are out in low light and keep to the right on shared boardwalks, which are popular with walkers in the evenings. Carry a basic tool kit, a spare tube and a pump even on the guided tour. And watch for the cobbles in Óbidos and Sintra when they are wet.
