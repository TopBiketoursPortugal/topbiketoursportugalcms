Portugal is a small country with an unreasonable amount of cycling in it. In a single week you can roll along Atlantic beaches from Porto, climb into the terraced vineyards of the Douro, follow cobbled pilgrimage roads to Santiago de Compostela, cross the cork-oak plains of the Alentejo and finish on the cliffs of the Algarve. The distances are short, the roads are quiet, the sun is out roughly 300 days a year and the food at the end of every stage is reason enough to ride.

I have spent fifteen years drawing routes across this country, first for myself and then for the thousands of riders who have joined our tours. This guide is the long version of the conversation I have with every one of them before they book: where to go, when, what the roads are really like, how to get around, and what it costs.

## Why Portugal

Portugal has quietly become one of Europe's best cycling destinations, and the reasons are structural rather than fashionable.

**The climate does most of the work.** On the western edge of the Iberian peninsula, Portugal gets an Atlantic-Mediterranean climate that is temperate almost everywhere. Winters are mild; only the far north and the high mountains see regular frost. Summers are warm and dry, with a sea breeze on the coast that keeps afternoons rideable. The rainy season runs from late November to February, and even then the south stays mostly dry. In practice the riding season runs from March to November in the north and all twelve months in the Algarve and Alentejo.

**The roads are quiet.** Portugal built a modern motorway network in the 1990s and 2000s, and traffic moved onto it. The old national roads (the *estradas nacionais*, N-numbered) and the dense web of municipal roads that connect every village were left to farmers, locals and cyclists. Outside Porto and Lisbon it is normal to ride for an hour on good tarmac and be passed by a handful of cars. Drivers are used to cyclists along the touring corridors, and the law requires 1.5 metres when overtaking.

**The variety is out of proportion to the size.** Portugal is roughly 560 km from north to south and 220 km wide. Into that it packs a green, mountainous north shaped by Celtic and Roman history; the oldest demarcated wine region in the world; the Serra da Estrela, near 2,000 m; nearly 1,800 km of coastline, counting the islands; a vast, flat, hot interior of wheat, olives and cork oaks; and a southern coast that is the sunniest corner of Europe. The four great rivers, the Minho, Douro, Mondego and Tejo, each carve a different landscape and each has a cycling route that follows it.

**The infrastructure has caught up.** The Algarve has the Ecovia do Litoral, a signed 214 km coast-to-coast cycle route. The interior has a growing set of *Ecopistas*, greenways on disused railway lines that are flat, car-free and perfect for families. EuroVelo 1 is signed along most of the west coast, the Camino is waymarked with yellow arrows, and hotels along every major corridor know what a touring cyclist needs: secure storage, an early breakfast, somewhere to rinse kit.

**It is affordable and safe.** Accommodation, meals and bike hire cost less than in France, Italy or the Alps, and the welcome is genuine. Portugal sits near the top of the Global Peace Index year after year, violent crime is rare, healthcare is accessible and the emergency number (112) works in English. For a first cycling holiday in Europe, or a first solo one, it is the country I recommend most often.

**And it is not flat.** I say this early because the brochures rarely do. The coast is flat to rolling. The Alentejo rolls gently. Everything else has hills, and the Douro has very steep ones. Choose the right corner of the country at the right time of year and the hills are a pleasure; choose wrong and you will remember the heat more than the views. For a quick calibration of what easy, moderate and challenging mean here, the [Cycling Portugal difficulty guide](/blog/cycling-portugal-difficulty-guide/) breaks it down by daily distance and elevation.

## The seven cycling regions

For a cyclist, seven areas matter, and they are different enough that most riders pick one or two per trip. From north to south:

### Minho & the North

The Minho is the green, wet, granite corner between Porto and the Spanish border, bounded by the Minho river to the north and threaded by the Lima and Cávado. This is the oldest Portugal: the country was founded at Guimarães, Braga is its religious capital, and every hillside is divided into small farms where *vinho verde* vines climb pergolas. Peneda-Gerês, the only national park, fills the north-eastern corner.

**Terrain.** The coast from Porto to Caminha is flat, with cycle paths and boardwalks for long stretches. The Lima and Minho valleys are flat too; our [North of Portugal and Minho tour](/tours/north-of-portugal-en/) follows their banks and is almost level despite the mountains around it. Leave the valleys and it gets steep fast: the roads into Gerês and the Serra d'Arga are proper climbs.

**Signature roads.** The Atlantic path from Vila do Conde to Viana do Castelo; the Ecovia do Minho between Valença and Caminha; the roads through Ponte de Lima, the oldest town in Portugal; the climb to the Portela do Homem pass in Gerês if you want a mountain.

**Who it suits.** Anyone who likes green, rural, historical landscapes and does not mind a shower outside July and August. Summer temperatures are the most comfortable in the country and the food is superb. Region page: [Porto and the North](/tours/regions/porto/). Deeper reading: [Northern Portugal bike tours](/blog/northern-portugal-bike-tours/) and [Minho vs Douro](/blog/minho-vs-douro-cycling/).

### Porto

Porto is the gateway to everything in the north and deserves a day or two of its own. The old centre is a hill city with cobbled streets, better walked than ridden, but the Ribeira waterfront, the Port lodges in Vila Nova de Gaia and the beach neighbourhood of Foz do Douro are an easy ride apart, and the seafront from Foz north through Matosinhos to Póvoa de Varzim is flat with a continuous cycle path.

**Who it suits.** City breakers, families, anyone with a single day to ride, and anyone arriving a day early for a longer tour. Our [Porto sightseeing ride](/tours/bike-tour-sightseeing-porto/), its [e-bike version](/tours/electric-bike-tour-porto/), the [food and wine ride](/tours/food-tour-bike-tour-wine-gastournomy-en/) and the [full day to Póvoa de Varzim](/tours/biketour-porto-deluxe/) all start in the centre. Region page: [Porto and the North](/tours/regions/porto/).

### Douro Valley

Ninety minutes east of Porto the river narrows, the valley walls rise and the landscape turns into the thing on the postcards: hand-built schist terraces (*socalcos*) climbing hundreds of metres from the water, planted with the vines that make Port and, increasingly, some of Portugal's best dry reds. The Alto Douro has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2001 and is the oldest demarcated wine region in the world.

**Terrain.** Steep. The river sits at about 100 m and the ridge roads run at 500–700 m, so every time you leave the water you climb, often in double-figure gradients. Distances are short, 40–70 km a day, but a 45 km stage can carry 1,000 m of ascent, and July afternoons pass 40 °C in the valley. This is the most physically demanding region in Portugal on a conventional bike and one of the easiest to enjoy on an e-bike.

**Signature roads.** The N222 from Peso da Régua to Pinhão, often voted one of the best driving roads in the world and just as good on a bike at dawn; the climb from Pinhão to Casal de Loivos; the river road east to Tua and the rock-art valley at Foz Côa. Further east, Trás-os-Montes and the Douro International canyon are wilder and emptier still.

**Who it suits.** Riders who value scenery over speed and are fit, on an e-bike, or both; wine lovers, because dinner at a *quinta* with the estate's own bottles is the best food-and-wine cycling in the country. Go in April–May for almond blossom and cool climbing, or September–October for the *vindima*, the harvest. Region page: [Douro river](/tours/regions/douro-river/). Tours: the [8-day, 270 km Douro Valley tour](/tours/bike-tours-douro-valley/), the [7-day easy version](/tours/bike-tours-douro-valley-easy/), the [Trás-os-Montes and Douro International tour](/tours/bike-tours-portugal-douro-wine/) for the hardest riding we offer, and a [single day from Porto](/tours/douro-valley-full-day-bike-ride/). Guide: [Cycling the Douro Valley](/guides/cycling-the-douro-valley/).

### Centro and the Silver Coast

Central Portugal is the region most visitors skip, which is why it rides so well. It holds three landscapes: the Silver Coast (*Costa de Prata*) of long beaches, lagoons and fishing towns; Coimbra, the old university city on the Mondego; and, inland, the schist villages and the Serra da Estrela, the highest range on the mainland.

**Terrain.** The coast is flat to gently rolling and cooler in summer than anywhere south. Aveiro sits on a lagoon of canals and salt pans with cycle paths in every direction. The old railway from Coimbra to Aveiro and the Ecopista do Dão near Viseu give long, car-free, level rides. Inland the N339 climbs to the Torre at 1,993 m, the biggest paved ascent in Portugal and a regular in the Volta a Portugal.

**Who it suits.** Beginners and intermediates who want coast and countryside in one trip, families on the rail trails, and anyone avoiding crowds even in August. Our [Silver Coast tour from Porto to Coimbra](/tours/porto-to-coimbra-biketour/), the [Coimbra–Aveiro e-bike loop](/tours/electric-biking-coimbra-to-aveiro-loop/) and the [Atlantic coast to Lisbon tour](/tours/atlantic-coast-to-lisbon-bike-tour/) all live here. Centro is covered by the [Porto](/tours/regions/porto/) and [Lisbon](/tours/regions/lisbon/) region pages; deeper reading in [Central Portugal bike tours](/blog/central-portugal-bike-tours/).

### Lisbon & Sintra

Lisbon is the hilliest capital in western Europe and not, in its centre, a cycling city. The region around it is superb. The Tagus riverside path runs flat for 30 km to Cascais; from there the coast road passes Guincho beach and climbs into the Serra de Sintra, a granite ridge of mist-fed forest topped with the palaces that make the town a UNESCO site, with Cabo da Roca, the westernmost point of mainland Europe, on the way. North of Sintra the surf coast continues past Ericeira and Peniche to Nazaré; south across the river is the Arrábida natural park and the start of the Alentejo.

**Terrain.** Riverside and the Cascais–Guincho coast are flat. Sintra is a proper climb from any side, 300–450 m on cobbles in places, with one of the best descents in the country back to the Atlantic. The coast north rolls constantly into a persistent summer north wind.

**Who it suits.** Day riders based in Lisbon, riders finishing Porto–Lisbon who want a strong last day, and anyone wanting a mild, quiet winter base. Region page: [Bike tours in Lisbon](/tours/regions/lisbon/). Guide: [Porto to Lisbon by bike](/guides/porto-to-lisbon-by-bike/).

### Alentejo

South of the Tagus the country opens out. The Alentejo is a third of Portugal's area with about five percent of its people: rolling plains of wheat and cork oak, olive groves, whitewashed villages with blue-trimmed doors, hilltop castles on the Spanish frontier and the wine country around Évora, Reguengos, Borba and Estremoz. Its coast, the Costa Vicentina, is a wild, cliff-lined shore protected as a natural park.

**Terrain.** Gentle. The interior rolls in long, easy waves; there are climbs to the castle towns (Marvão, Monsaraz, Castelo de Vide, Évoramonte) but nothing sustained, and the traffic is the lightest in the country. The challenge is climate, not gradient: in July and August the interior is routinely 38–42 °C with little shade. From October to May it is close to perfect, and sunny winter days of 15 °C are normal.

**Signature roads.** Évora to Monsaraz across the Alqueva reservoir; the lanes between Estremoz, Borba and Vila Viçosa through marble quarries and vineyards; the road up to Marvão; the old N2 across the plain; on the coast, the Historical Way of the Rota Vicentina.

**Who it suits.** Food and wine travellers, riders who want quiet above all, and anyone travelling between October and May. Our [Alentejo wine country and castles tour](/tours/biketour-in-alentejo-wine-country-and-castles/) and its [extended version](/tours/more-of-biking-alentejo-wine-country-and-castles/) are the classic itineraries. Region page: [Cycling tours in Alentejo](/tours/regions/alentejo/). Deeper reading: [Cycling Alentejo](/blog/cycling-alentejo-the-complete-bike-tour-guide/) and [Best time to cycle in Alentejo](/blog/best-time-to-cycle-in-alentejo/).

### Algarve

The southern coast is Portugal's best-known region and its most reliable cycling climate: mild winters, long sunny days and the least rain in the country. There are three Algarves: the sandy, lagoon-fringed east from the Spanish border to Faro, with Tavira and the Ria Formosa; the busier central coast of cliffs and coves from Albufeira to Lagos; and the wild west from Sagres north to Aljezur, where the Atlantic takes over. Behind all of it is the *barrocal* of almond, carob and fig, and the Serra de Monchique at 900 m.

**Terrain.** The Ecovia do Litoral runs the full 214 km of the coast on cycle paths, quiet roads and compact gravel, mostly flat. The west coast rolls more and takes the wind. Monchique and the inland loops are hilly and, in spring, some of the best road riding in the country; the professional peloton races here every February in the Volta ao Algarve for exactly that reason.

**Signature roads.** The Ecovia between Tavira and Faro through the Ria Formosa; Lagos to Sagres and out to Cabo de São Vicente; the climb to Fóia on Monchique; the west coast road from Sagres to Aljezur.

**Who it suits.** Beginners and families on the Ecovia; anyone travelling in winter; road cyclists in February and March; riders who want south and west coasts in one tour, which is what our [Vicentine Coast and Algarve tour](/tours/south-portugal-biketour/) does over 8 days and 378 km. Region page: [Cycling tours in Algarve](/tours/regions/algarve/). Guide: [Cycling the Algarve](/guides/cycling-the-algarve/). Deeper reading: [Southern Portugal bike tours](/blog/southern-portugal-bike-tours/) and the [Algarve seasons guide](/blog/algarve-cycling-seasons-guide/).

## Portugal's great long-distance routes

Most riders do not want a region; they want a line on a map with a beginning and an end. These six routes give Portugal its shape for a touring cyclist. The route profile cards above give the numbers; here is what each is actually like.

### The N2, Chaves to Faro

The Estrada Nacional 2 is the only road that crosses the entire country: 739 km from Chaves near the northern border to Faro on the south coast. The Portuguese have adopted it as their Route 66, with kilometre markers, a passport stamped in each of the 35 municipalities it crosses and a growing set of hotels and cafés for people travelling it slowly.

On a bike it is a tour of the whole country: over the Serra do Marão to Vila Real, down to the Douro at Peso da Régua (one of the best descents in Portugal), across the Dão wine country to Viseu and the schist hills around Sertã, then the long flat Alentejo through Ponte de Sor, Montemor-o-Novo and Almodôvar, and a last climb over the Serra do Caldeirão before the descent to Faro.

It is fully paved, almost entirely on the old national road, with light traffic except near the larger towns. The difficulty is the accumulated climbing, around 9,000–10,000 m depending on how you measure it, and the Alentejo heat if you ride in summer. Ten to twelve days is a sensible pace. Ride north to south so the big climbs come early and the prevailing summer wind helps you home; April, May, September and October are the months.

### The Atlantic Coast, Porto to Lisbon

The classic Portuguese tour and the one we sell most: roughly 350–400 km along the Green Coast and Silver Coast from the Douro to the Tagus with the ocean on your right almost the whole way. The full uncut version we run takes [13 days and 493 km](/tours/porto-to-lisbon-bike-tour/); shorter versions skip the industrial belt south of Porto by train and ride the best of it in a week.

The stages tell the story: Porto to Espinho on the seafront; boardwalks and dune paths to Ovar and Aveiro's canals; the empty beach road through Mira and Tocha to Figueira da Foz; the pine forests of the Pinhal de Leiria to Nazaré, where the biggest waves in the world break beneath the cliff in winter; Alcobaça and the walled town of Óbidos; Peniche; the surf coast of Santa Cruz and Ericeira; and the finale through Sintra, Cabo da Roca, Guincho and Cascais to the riverside ride into Lisbon.

It is flat to gently rolling, graded 2 out of 5, and the only real climb is Sintra on the last day. Daily distances of 40–65 km leave time for beaches. The northerly *nortada* wind is why everyone rides it north to south. It works from April to October and is best in May, June and September. The full breakdown is in the [Porto to Lisbon guide](/guides/porto-to-lisbon-by-bike/) and the [Atlantic coast article](/blog/portugal-atlantic-coast-bike-tour/).

### The Portuguese Camino, Porto to Santiago de Compostela

The route this company was founded on, and the one I still think is the most beautiful Camino of the several I have ridden. From Porto cathedral there are two ways to Santiago. The [Coastal Way](/tours/porto-santiago-compostela-bike-tour-coastal-way/) follows the Atlantic through Vila do Conde, Esposende, Viana do Castelo and Caminha, takes the little ferry across the Minho to A Guarda, and continues up the Galician coast through Baiona, Vigo and Pontevedra before turning inland for the last two days: about 278 km over 7–8 days, easy to moderate. The [Central Way](/tours/santiago-de-compostela-central-way/) goes inland through Barcelos, Ponte de Lima and Valença, crosses to Tui and joins the coastal route at Redondela; it is slightly shorter, hillier and more historic, with the Roman road and the Labruja climb as its signature moments.

Both are waymarked with yellow arrows, both qualify for the Compostela certificate (you must ride at least the last 200 km), and both mix tarmac with cobble, boardwalk and forest track that suit a hybrid, gravel or touring bike. Daily distances of 35–50 km leave time for the churches, the pilgrim menus and the company of walkers. Spring and autumn are ideal; late summer is busiest and most atmospheric. The [Camino guide](/guides/cycling-the-portuguese-camino/) covers stages, credentials and the coastal-versus-central choice. For the much longer French Way see our [León](/tours/bike-tour-camino-de-santiago-cycling-french-way-leon/) and [full French Way](/tours/french-way-to-santiago-bike-tour/) tours.

### The Douro loop

There is no single signed Douro route; the valley is too steep and fragmented. What works is a loop out of Porto that climbs into the valley, rides its best roads on both banks between Régua, Pinhão and Tua, and uses the river train (the *Linha do Douro*, one of the great railway journeys of Europe) to shortcut the flat, busy stretch back to the city. Our [8-day, 270 km version](/tours/bike-tours-douro-valley/) is the most demanding itinerary we run, around 5,500 m of climbing at gradients that regularly touch 12–15%; the [7-day easy version](/tours/bike-tours-douro-valley-easy/) stays lower, rides 133 km and uses the train more.

What you get for the effort: the N222 at dawn before the tour cars arrive; the viewpoints at Galafura and Casal de Loivos; tastings at *quintas* that have made Port for two centuries; Lamego; and, if you push east, the rock-art valley at Foz Côa. E-bikes are on every departure and turn the Douro from a 4 out of 5 into something most active people can enjoy. Go in April–May or September–October. Everything else is in the [Douro guide](/guides/cycling-the-douro-valley/).

### The Ecopista do Dão and the rail trails

Portugal closed most of its narrow-gauge railways in the 1980s and 1990s and has been converting the trackbeds into *Ecopistas*: flat, traffic-free greenways that are the best family cycling in the country. The longest and best known is the Ecopista do Dão, 49 km from Viseu to Santa Comba Dão through vineyards and pine forest. Others worth a day: the Ecopista do Tâmega from Amarante, used by our [Douro Valley full-day ride](/tours/douro-valley-full-day-bike-ride/); the Ecopista do Minho between Monção and Valença; and the Guadiana greenway in the south. The [Coimbra to Aveiro loop](/tours/electric-biking-coimbra-to-aveiro-loop/) strings together canal towpaths, lagoon paths and rail trail for a four-day tour with almost no traffic.

### The Vicentine Coast

The south-western corner, where the Alentejo coast meets the western Algarve, is the wildest shoreline in the country: 100 km of cliffs, empty beaches and fishing villages inside the Parque Natural do Sudoeste Alentejano e Costa Vicentina. The Rota Vicentina is the trail network through it. Its famous Fishermen's Trail hugs the cliff edge on sand and is strictly for walkers; the Historical Way, a few kilometres inland on farm tracks and quiet roads, is the cycling route, with dedicated cycling stages published by the Rota Vicentina association. Porto Covo, Vila Nova de Milfontes, Zambujeira do Mar, Odeceixe, Aljezur and Sagres are the stage towns. It is rolling rather than mountainous, exposed to the wind and rideable almost all year; our [Vicentine Coast and Algarve tour](/tours/south-portugal-biketour/) combines it with the Ecovia along the south coast.

Also worth knowing: **EuroVelo 1** runs the length of Portugal and overlaps much of the Porto–Lisbon and Vicentine routes, with patchy but improving signing.

## When to go: month by month

The short answer has not changed in fifteen years: **April to June and September to October** are the best months for cycling in Portugal everywhere, and **November to March** is good in the Algarve and Alentejo. The long answer is that the country is tall enough, and its coast-to-interior contrast sharp enough, that every month is the right month somewhere. The climate table above gives the numbers; here is how the year feels on a bike.

**January and February.** The quietest months. The Algarve and Alentejo sit at 10–18 °C by day with more sun than rain; you want arm warmers in the morning and sun cream by noon. Lisbon is similar but wetter. The north is cool and wet and the Serra da Estrela has snow. The pros open their season in the Volta ao Algarve in February. Daylight is about ten hours.

**March.** The Algarve is in almond and orange blossom and this is its best month: warm, green and not yet busy. The Alentejo is at its greenest. The north is still changeable.

**April.** The whole country opens. The Douro is in blossom with cool climbing temperatures, the Minho is green and the rivers are full. Easter week is busy on the Camino. Spring temperatures run 18–26 °C.

**May.** My favourite month almost anywhere. Long days, wildflowers, warm but not hot, and the crowds still weeks away. The Alentejo is at its best; the Atlantic coast and the Camino are ideal; the Douro is lush before the sun turns it gold.

**June.** Still excellent, with light until past nine in the evening. Inland temperatures start to climb and the Alentejo and Douro see their first 35 °C afternoons. Coast, Minho and Centro are perfect. São João on 23 June turns Porto into a street party.

**July and August.** Peak summer, peak heat. The coast is wonderful: 25–30 °C, a sea breeze, long days and the *nortada* pushing you south. The Minho, the Silver Coast, Porto–Lisbon and the Coastal Camino all work. The interior is another matter: the Douro, Alentejo and the N2 south of Abrantes routinely reach 38–42 °C, and we do not recommend them in these months unless you start at first light and finish by one. August is when the Portuguese go on holiday, so beach towns are full and accommodation should be booked well ahead. The Volta a Portugal runs for ten days in early August.

**September.** The second great month. The sea is at its warmest, crowds thin after the first week, the interior cools into the high twenties and the *vindima* starts in the Douro and Alentejo: grapes on the roadside, tractors on the lanes, harvest lunches at the *quintas*. Everything is rideable.

**October.** Still warm in the south (Algarve 22–25 °C), mild in the north, with the first autumn rain in the Minho late in the month. The Douro is at its most beautiful, gold and red after the harvest. Daylight shortens after the clocks change.

**November and December.** The rainy season settles over the north and centre, in fronts rather than constant drizzle, and short days make these the months to avoid there. The Algarve, the Alentejo and Lisbon remain good at 15–20 °C and quiet, with Christmas week the only busy one.

Three rules follow. If you can only travel in July or August, stay on the coast. If you want the interior, go in spring or autumn. If you want to cycle in a European winter, the Algarve is the most reliable place on the continent to do it. Region-specific detail is in the [Algarve seasons guide](/blog/algarve-cycling-seasons-guide/) and [best time to cycle in Alentejo](/blog/best-time-to-cycle-in-alentejo/).

**Wind, which nobody warns you about.** Temperature gets the attention but wind decides how a day feels on the Portuguese coast. From late spring to early autumn the *nortada*, a steady northerly, blows down the west coast most afternoons at 20–40 km/h. Ride south and it is a gift; ride north into it and a flat 60 km day becomes hard work. It is the single biggest reason every Atlantic itinerary we run goes Porto to Lisbon and not the reverse, and why we start coastal days early. The Algarve's south coast is sheltered from it; the west coast from Sagres north is not.

## Road surface, traffic and safety

Portugal is one of the safest countries in Europe for cycling, and I say that having ridden most of the alternatives. But "safe" is a function of route choice, and it is worth understanding what the roads are really like.

**Road types.** Motorways (A-numbered) are closed to bikes. The old national roads (N-numbered) carry most of the remaining through traffic; some are busy near cities, most are quiet in the countryside, and almost all have a paved shoulder. Below them is an enormous network of municipal roads and unnumbered lanes connecting every village and carrying almost no traffic. Good route design in Portugal means linking the municipal roads and using the N roads only where there is no alternative. This is what we spend our winters doing.

**Surfaces.** The tarmac is generally good. Exceptions: historic town centres are cobbled, sometimes with *calçada portuguesa*, the small limestone setts that are slippery when wet; the Camino and the Ecovia include cobble, boardwalk and compact gravel; rural lanes in the Alentejo and Minho have rough patches. A hybrid, touring or gravel bike on 32–40 mm tyres handles all of it. A road bike is fine for the N2, the Douro and the Algarve inland roads, less comfortable on the Camino. The [complete guide to bicycles](/blog/complete-guide-to-bicycles/) matches bike to route.

**Drivers.** Outside the two big cities Portuguese drivers are used to cyclists and generally patient, particularly along the established touring corridors. The law requires a 1.5 m overtaking gap and forbids passing a cyclist on a blind bend; most drivers observe it. The places to be alert are the approaches to Porto and Lisbon, the Algarve's EN125 in summer (we avoid it entirely; the Ecovia runs parallel) and the N roads around Aveiro and Leiria that carry lorries. On a well-designed route you will spend a few percent of your time on any of them.

**Rules worth knowing.** Helmets are not compulsory for adults but we require them on every tour. Lights are compulsory after dark. Cyclists may ride two abreast. The drink-drive limit applies to cyclists.

**Heat.** Inland in summer, heat is the main hazard, not traffic. Start early, carry two bottles, refill at every café, and stop for the hottest hours. Heat sends more of our riders to a pharmacy than crashes do.

**Emergencies.** Dial 112; operators speak English. Pharmacies (green cross) are everywhere, every town of any size has a health centre and the major towns have hospitals. EU and UK visitors should carry their EHIC or GHIC card; everyone should have travel insurance that covers cycling. Our guided tours include insurance; on self-guided tours bring your own.

**Guided or self-guided, from a safety angle.** A guide who knows the road and can call the van is the safest format for nervous first-timers or mixed groups. A well-supported self-guided tour, with GPS tracks, a roadbook and a support number that answers, is very safe too; thousands of riders do it every year. What matters far more than the format is the route.

## Getting there and around

**Flights.** Three mainland airports matter to a cyclist. **Porto** (OPO) is 15 minutes from the centre by metro and is the gateway for the Minho, the Douro, the Camino and the Atlantic coast heading south, with direct flights from most UK and European cities and several North American hubs. **Lisbon** (LIS) is the main intercontinental airport, with direct flights from the US east coast and Canada, and serves Sintra, the Alentejo and the southern end of the Atlantic route. **Faro** (FAO) is the Algarve's airport, busy with UK and Irish flights all year and the obvious choice for the Ecovia, the Vicentine coast and the Alentejo from the south. All three have taxis and transfers that take a boxed bike if you tell the driver in advance.

**Bringing your own bike.** Every airline flying into Portugal carries bikes in a box, for a fee that varies wildly; most Porto and Lisbon bike shops will store an empty box. For a one- or two-week tour we generally suggest renting: a well-maintained hire bike fitted to you is included in every tour we run. Bring your own pedals, saddle and shoes if you care about them.

**Trains.** CP (Comboios de Portugal) is the national operator and genuinely useful. Broadly: on regional, inter-regional and urban trains (including the Porto and Lisbon suburban networks and the Douro line) an assembled bike travels free outside weekday peak hours in the designated space; on the long-distance Intercidades and Alfa Pendular services bikes go either bagged as luggage or in a small number of reservable bike spaces, depending on the train. Capacity is limited and policies change, so check CP's current bicycle page before relying on it and reserve the bike space with your seat where you can.

The lines that matter: **Porto–Lisbon** (about three hours) for returning from the Atlantic route; the **Douro line** from Porto São Bento to Régua, Pinhão and Pocinho, the backbone of every Douro tour; the **Minho line** to Viana do Castelo and Valença for the Camino; **Lisbon–Faro** and the **Algarve line** from Lagos to Vila Real de Santo António, the full length of the Ecovia; and **Lisbon–Évora** for the Alentejo. There is no railway into Trás-os-Montes or the Serra da Estrela.

**Buses and ferries.** Long-distance buses usually take a boxed or bagged bike in the hold, not an assembled one. The small ferries across the Minho at Caminha, the Tagus at Lisbon and the Sado at Setúbal all take bikes.

**Transfers and support.** Every multi-day tour we run includes daily luggage transfer between hotels: you leave your bag at reception in the morning and it is in your room when you arrive. Airport and point-to-point transfers are included or available on all of them, and on guided tours the support van follows the group. Porto, Lisbon, Faro, Lagos and Tavira have good rental outfits and workshops; the Douro, Alentejo and Minho do not, which is one reason to arrive with a tour-supplied bike and a support number.

## Guided vs self-guided vs day tours

The comparison table above summarises it; this is the reasoning behind it.

**Guided tours** put a local guide on the road with you every day, a support van behind the group and a fixed group on a fixed departure date. The guide navigates, fixes punctures, knows which café has the best *pastel de nata* and which *quinta* will open a bottle for a small group, and manages pace so that a mixed-fitness group stays together (the e-bike is the great leveller here). Most meals and insurance are included. It costs more because of all that, and it suits first-timers, solo travellers who want company, groups of friends of different abilities and anyone for whom navigating a foreign country by bike is more stress than fun. Our [guided bike tours guide](/blog/guided-bike-tours-in-portugal/) describes a guided week in detail.

**Self-guided tours** use the same hotels, bikes, luggage transfers and route as the guided version, but you ride on your own schedule: start any day of the week, ride at your pace, stop when you like, eat where you like. You get GPS tracks, a printed roadbook, a briefing at the start and a phone number that reaches a real person who can send a mechanic or a van. Most of our riders choose this format. It suits couples and small groups who want a planned trip without a group, experienced cyclists who want independence with a safety net, and anyone whose dates do not fit a fixed departure. The [self-guided guide](/guides/self-guided-bike-tours-portugal/) and the [self-guided versus guided comparison](/blog/self-guided-vs-guided-bike-tours-in-portugal/) weigh it from both sides.

**Day tours** are three hours to a full day from Porto or Lisbon: the seafront, the river, the Douro by train and bike. No luggage, no hotels, a guide and a good bike. They suit city breaks, families with younger children and people testing whether a multi-day tour is for them. Several riders each year do a [Porto day ride](/tours/bike-tour-sightseeing-porto/), enjoy it and book the Camino for the following spring.

**Fully independent touring**, carrying your own kit and booking your own hotels, is perfectly possible; the N2 and the Camino both have a strong culture of it. Cheaper, heavier, more planning, no support.

**How fit do you need to be?** Less than you think. Easy tours run 30–55 km a day on flat terrain; moderate ones 55–75 km with rolling hills; challenging ones 75–100+ km with real climbing or heat. If you can ride 40 km on a Saturday without it ruining your Sunday, you can ride the Atlantic coast, the Algarve or the Camino. An e-bike moves any route at least one grade easier, which is why couples of different fitness so often ride one each.

## Events calendar

Portugal's cycling calendar follows the climate. The season opens on the south coast in February, moves north through spring with a series of *granfondos* (mass-participation sportives on marshalled roads), pauses for the heat in July and August except for the national tour, and finishes with the Douro and Algarve events in autumn. The list above comes from our events data and is updated each winter; the headlines:

- **February:** the Volta ao Algarve, a five-day professional race that brings the WorldTour to the Algarve's climbs and is easy to combine with a week of riding the same roads.
- **Spring (March–June):** granfondo season in the Algarve, the Lisbon region, the Serra da Estrela and the north, including L'Étape Portugal by Tour de France near Lisbon.
- **August:** the Volta a Portugal, the national stage race, ten days across the country, usually via the Serra da Estrela.
- **September–October:** Douro granfondos during the harvest and the autumn Algarve events as the weather cools.

If you plan to ride during a major event, book accommodation early; stage towns fill up. If you plan to avoid events, the same list tells you which roads will be closed when.

## Costs and budgeting

Portugal is one of the cheaper countries in western Europe to cycle, though no longer the bargain it was a decade ago, and Lisbon, Porto and the Algarve in August now price like the rest of Europe. Everything here is in euros; the dollar and sterling rates move, so treat the ratios as more reliable than the figures and check a converter before you budget.

**On the road.** A coffee and a pastry in a village café cost a euro or two; a *prato do dia* set lunch with soup, a main course, a drink and coffee in a local *tasca* is the best-value meal in Europe and rarely exceeds the low teens; dinner in a good regional restaurant with wine is moderate by British or American standards; a bottle of decent local wine from a shop costs less than a single glass at home. A clean guesthouse along the main routes is reasonable and cheaper inland than on the coast; a night at a Douro *quinta* or a Pousada is a justified splurge.

**Tours.** A self-guided multi-day tour with a quality bike, pre-booked hotels, breakfasts, daily luggage transfer, GPS navigation and phone support sits in the middle of the European range, well below the Alps, Tuscany or Provence for the equivalent standard. Guided tours cost more because they add a guide, a support vehicle, most meals and insurance; e-bikes carry a supplement. Prices for every tour are on the tour pages and include what is listed there; flights, lunches and dinners on self-guided tours, and travel insurance typically are not. Our [terms and conditions](/terms-and-conditions/) spell out the detail.

**Where the money goes.** Accommodation is the biggest line on any tour, followed by bikes and transfers. The biggest saving most people can make is on dates: shoulder-season rates in the Algarve and on the Silver Coast are far below August, and spring and autumn are better for riding anyway.

**Tipping and cash.** Tipping is modest: round up a café bill and leave five to ten percent for good restaurant service. Cards are accepted almost everywhere; carry some cash for the smallest cafés and the Camino, where some *albergues* are cash-only.

## Food and wine on the road

A cycling holiday burns a lot of calories, and Portugal is one of the best countries in the world to replace them. The food changes with every region you ride through, which is part of the point of riding through them.

**In the Minho and the north** the table is hearty: *caldo verde* (kale and potato soup with a slice of *chouriço*), *rojões* (braised pork), *bacalhau* in any of its supposed 365 preparations, sardines on the coast, and *francesinha*, Porto's meat-and-cheese sandwich under a beer-and-tomato sauce, which is exactly what you want after 70 km. The wine is *vinho verde*: young, slightly fizzy, low in alcohol and made for lunchtime. Porto has one of the best eating scenes in Europe, and our [food and wine ride](/tours/food-tour-bike-tour-wine-gastournomy-en/) is built around it.

**In the Douro** you eat at the *quintas*: roast kid, *posta mirandesa* (a huge steak from the cattle of Trás-os-Montes), river fish, almond cake, and the estate's own wines, which means Port at the end of the meal and serious dry reds through it. The harvest adds grapes, figs and the *vindima* lunches laid on for the pickers.

**In the Centro** the coast is about fish: grilled bass and bream, *caldeirada* fish stew, *ovos moles* egg-yolk sweets in Aveiro, and the *leitão*, suckling pig, that the Bairrada region between Coimbra and Aveiro is famous for, paired with its sparkling wine. Inland, the Serra da Estrela gives the country its best cheese, a runny sheep's-milk *queijo da Serra* sold in every village on the N2 between Viseu and the Zêzere.

**In the Alentejo** the cooking is the most distinctive in Portugal and, for many riders, the best: bread-based *açorda* and *migas*, black pork (*porco preto*) from pigs that eat the acorns under the cork oaks you have been riding past all day, lamb stews, sheep's cheese from Serpa and Nisa, the *sericaia* egg pudding with Elvas plums, and wines from Reguengos, Borba, Estremoz and Vidigueira that are rich, ripe and cheap. The [food lover's guide](/blog/food-lovers-guide-portugal/) goes deeper.

**In the Algarve** it is seafood: *cataplana* (clams, fish or pork and clams cooked in a copper clam-shaped pot), grilled sardines and octopus, *arroz de marisco* seafood rice, and the almond and fig sweets of the *barrocal*.

**On the bike.** Portuguese cafés are the touring cyclist's friend: every village has one, they open early, a *galão* (milky coffee) and a *tosta mista* cost very little, and nobody minds Lycra. Tap water is safe everywhere. Lunch is the big meal; sit down at noon and you are back on the bike before the afternoon heat. Dinner starts late, rarely before 8 pm.

## Practical FAQ

The accordion below answers the six questions we are asked most often. A few more that come up every season:

**What should I pack?** Less than you think. Two sets of cycling kit, a rain jacket (yes, even for the Algarve), a warm layer for spring and autumn evenings, sun cream, a small first-aid kit, your own pedals and saddle if you are particular, and ordinary clothes for the evening; hotels along the routes will wash kit. The full checklist is in the [packing list](/blog/ultimate-packing-list-for-a-bike-tour-in-portugal/).

**Do I need to train?** For easy and moderate routes, a few 40–60 km rides in the month before you travel make the first days comfortable. For the Douro and the N2 on a conventional bike, treat it as an event and follow the [multi-day training guide](/blog/multi-day-bike-tour-training-guide/).

**Can I bring children?** Yes; the rail trails, the Algarve Ecovia and the Minho river valleys are ideal. We provide children's bikes, child seats, tag-alongs and trailers on request; see [cycling with kids in Portugal](/blog/cycling-with-kids-portugal/).

**What about older riders?** A large share of our guests are over 60, and e-bikes have made every route we run accessible to anyone who is active; the [seniors' guide](/blog/cycling-holidays-in-portugal-for-seniors/) has the specifics.

**Which bike?** Hybrid or touring for the coast and the Camino; gravel for the Ecopistas and farm tracks; road for the N2, the Douro and the Algarve inland; e-bike for hills you would rather enjoy than suffer. See the [road](/blog/road-bike-in-portugal/), [gravel](/blog/gravel-bike-in-portugal/) and [touring](/blog/touring-bike-in-portugal/) guides.

**Where do I start?** Pick the region that matches the landscape you picture yourself in, the month that suits it, and the format that matches how you like to travel. Then read the relevant guide: [Porto to Lisbon](/guides/porto-to-lisbon-by-bike/), [the Portuguese Camino](/guides/cycling-the-portuguese-camino/), [the Douro](/guides/cycling-the-douro-valley/), [the Algarve](/guides/cycling-the-algarve/) or [self-guided touring](/guides/self-guided-bike-tours-portugal/). If you are still not sure, write to us; we have been having this conversation for fifteen years and we enjoy it.
